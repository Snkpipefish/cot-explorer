Reading Guide · for first-time readers

How to read this almanac

The dashboard looks dense at first because it pulls together a lot of separate market signals into one view. You do not have to understand every column. This page walks through the parts in plain English, in the order most readers find useful. Bookmark it — there is no test.

I What you are looking at

The site is a quiet, daily snapshot of the futures markets — currencies, metals, oil, grains, softs (coffee, sugar, cocoa, cotton), bond yields, and a few crypto contracts. It does not tell you to buy or sell anything. It gives you a labelled view of where positioning is, what the macro backdrop looks like, and which markets are leaning bullish or bearish today.

Think of it the way a sailor thinks of an almanac: a reference you check before setting out, so you know where the tides and winds are likely to be.

II Reader Mode

The button in the top-right of the deck. Off by default for power users; on by default for first-time visitors. When on, the two most technical tabs (Engine Room and Data Health) are hidden, the tab labels switch to plain English, and the small italic explanations under each panel become shorter.

Nothing is deleted — turn it off any time you want the full view. Your choice is remembered between visits.

III The Today tab — your starting point

The first tab (Today) is where almost everyone should start. It opens with a single sentence summarising the day, followed by two short cards ("Top Bullish" and "Top Bearish"), and then a grid of one card per market.

The bull / bear gauge

Each card carries one number from −3 to +3:

−3 bearish +3 bullish
  • Around 0 — no clear lean. Most cards live here.
  • ±1 — a real tilt, worth noting.
  • ±2 or more — strong, multi-source agreement.

Click any card to open a deeper view of that market.

Rule of thumb Treat the gauge as a thermometer, not an order. It says "this market is leaning this way today", not "buy this now".

IV What is a COT report?

"COT" stands for Commitments of Traders. Every Friday, the US futures regulator publishes a snapshot of who owns what in the futures markets, split into a handful of groups:

  • Commercials — producers and consumers (an oil company, a wheat mill). They use futures to lock in prices and hedge their real-world business.
  • Managed money / large speculators — hedge funds and big traders. They are betting on price direction.
  • Small traders — everyone else.

When speculators are extremely crowded on one side, that is often interesting — crowded trades tend to unwind sharply. The dashboard flags these as extremes (top or bottom 10% of the past few years).

V Trade Ideas vs Engine Room

Two tabs cover the same underlying scoring, but at different levels of detail.

  • Trade Ideas shows only the observations that have passed the quality filter — labelled A or B by conviction. Read-only. Designed to be skimmable.
  • Engine Room exposes every score for every market and every time-horizon, including the ones that did not pass the filter. Useful for understanding why a market is or is not on the list — overkill for casual reading.

If you are not sure: stick with Trade Ideas. Engine Room will still be there when you are curious.

VI Data Health

The last tab simply shows whether each upstream data source has been refreshed recently. Green means recent, amber means aging, red means stale. If something on the main pages looks off, this is where you check whether the underlying feed is the problem.

G Glossary

Same definitions you see in the small popovers on the main page, gathered in one place.

COT report
Commitments of Traders. A weekly US government report listing who owns what in commodity and currency futures, split into hedgers, speculators and small traders. Source: CFTC, ICE, Euronext. Tuesday snapshot, published Friday.
Open interest
The total number of futures contracts that exist right now. Rising open interest means new money is coming in; falling means positions are being closed.
Percentile
Where today's number sits compared to the past few years. 0 = record low, 100 = record high. Above 90 or below 10 is usually called an extreme.
Extreme
Positioning that sits in the top or bottom 10% of its historical range. Crowded trades — they often unwind in sharp moves.
Bullish / Bearish
Tilted toward a rising / falling price.
Bull / bear gauge
A single number from −3 (strongly bearish) to +3 (strongly bullish) that combines positioning, macro, sector data and event risk into one reading per market.
Bedrock
The internal scoring engine behind the trade ideas. Tab V (Engine Room) lets you look under the hood. Most readers can skip it. ~96 drivers across 6 families (trend, positioning, macro, fundamental, risk, structure), asymmetric per direction and horizon.
Published vs near publish
A signal is "published" when its score clears the threshold the engine considers tradable. "Near publish" means it is close — worth watching, not yet ready. Threshold is per instrument and direction (min_score_publish).
Driver
A single piece of evidence — for example "positioning is at a 5-year extreme" or "the dollar is strengthening". Many drivers together form a signal.
Horizon
How long a signal is meant to last. Scalp = hours to a day. Swing = days to weeks. Macro = weeks to months.
Setup
A read-only observation about a market that has cleared the publish threshold. Each row is labelled A or B by conviction — not a trade order, just a view.
Affects
Which markets a given signal is currently bearing on. Click any tag to jump to that instrument in the Position Pressure tab.
ENSO
El Niño / La Niña — the Pacific ocean temperature cycle that swings global rainfall. Matters for grains, sugar, coffee and cocoa.
BDI
Baltic Dry Index — the price of moving bulk goods by sea. A real-time read on global trade demand.
VIX
The market's "fear gauge" — implied volatility of the S&P 500 over the next 30 days. Above 25 = stressed, below 15 = complacent.
DXY
A trade-weighted index of the US dollar against major peers. The single most important macro number for most markets.
WASDE
World Agricultural Supply and Demand Estimates. The monthly US government report that sets the agenda for grains and softs.
Score
The number the engine gives a signal after summing all its drivers. Higher is more conviction. Each instrument has its own publish threshold.
